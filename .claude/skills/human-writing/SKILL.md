# Write like a human, not like an AI

Before writing or editing any user-facing copy, check it against the patterns below. These are the most common tells that reveal text was AI-generated (compiled from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" guide: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing). Strip them out. If a draft already has them, rewrite the offending sentence rather than lightly editing it.

## Banned/suspect vocabulary

Avoid these words and phrases unless there's no natural alternative — they are overused filler that signal AI authorship:

`delve`, `boast(s)`, `tapestry`, `testament`, `underscore(s)`, `pivotal`, `crucial`, `vibrant`, `meticulous(ly)`, `intricate`, `interplay`, `landscape` (as in "the X landscape"), `garner`, `bolster`, `foster(ing)`, `enhance`, `showcase`, `nestled`, `groundbreaking`, `renowned`, `diverse array`, `robust`, `seamless(ly)`, `unlock`, `elevate`, `journey` (as a metaphor for a process/timeline), `ecosystem` (unless literally biological/technical), `holistic`.

## Sentence-construction patterns to avoid

- **Avoiding plain "is/are"**: don't replace simple copulas with "serves as," "stands as," "functions as," "represents," "marks." Just say what it is.
- **"Not just X, but Y"** and **"X, not Y"** constructions used purely for rhetorical flourish — cut them, state the point directly.
- **Rule of three**: reflexively listing three adjectives or three examples to sound thorough ("faster, simpler, and more reliable"). Use however many actually apply — often just one or two.
- **Undue significance statements**: sentences whose only job is to tell the reader something matters ("this reflects our commitment to...", "highlighting the importance of..."). Cut them; show significance through concrete detail instead, or don't state it at all.
- **Present-participle tacked-on analysis**: appending "-ing" phrases as pseudo-insight ("...ensuring a better experience," "...reflecting our values"). Delete or replace with a real, specific claim.
- **Outline-conclusion formula**: "Despite its [positives], it also faces [challenges]..." — don't force balance where none was asked for.
- **Promotional/travel-brochure tone**: don't write like an ad. State facts plainly; let specifics carry the persuasion.

## Formatting tells to avoid

- Title Case In Headings — use normal sentence case.
- Mechanical bolding of "key terms" throughout body text.
- Excessive em dashes used as a substitute for periods or commas — prefer normal punctuation, vary it.
- Emoji used as bullet/list markers.
- Inline bold-header-then-colon list items copy-pasted from a listicle format when normal prose or a real list would read better.

## What to do instead

- Vary sentence length and rhythm; don't make every sentence the same shape.
- Prefer concrete, specific claims over abstract statements of importance.
- Let word repetition happen when the plain word is clearest — don't force a synonym just for "lexical diversity."
- Cut any sentence that could be deleted without losing information.
- Read the draft out loud (mentally): if it sounds like ad copy or a LinkedIn post, rewrite it plainer.

## Applying this to edits

When asked to write or revise copy in this project:
1. Draft normally.
2. Scan the draft against the lists above.
3. Rewrite any flagged word/pattern — don't just swap the single word, restructure the sentence if the underlying construction is the problem (e.g. a "not just X but Y" sentence usually needs to be rebuilt, not patched).
4. Keep the result short and plain rather than padding it back out to a similar length.
